Lead-in 2 · Agree or disagree?

4:00Tap where you stand, then back it up with a reason and an example.

1 · History is the most useful subject at school.

Model answer

I don't agree. History is interesting, but maths, chemistry and biology are what got me into the academy, and I still use them every week. I'd call history the most meaningful subject, not the most useful.

2 · Old objects belong in museums, not in people's homes.

Model answer

I partly agree. Something like a royal crown or an ancient statue should be in a museum, where experts can look after it and everyone can see it. But my grandfather's old army compass would mean nothing in a glass case, and at home it means a lot to us.

3 · A good presenter never reads from notes.

Model answer

I disagree. In my pharmacology seminar I kept a small card with the drug doses on it, because getting a number wrong is far worse than glancing down for a second. Reading out every line is the problem, not checking one key fact.

4 · Every town needs its own museum.

Model answer

I agree, as long as it's small and cheap to run. My hometown has a two-room museum about the war, and that's where kids first see what their great-grandparents went through. A big museum in a city two hours away just can't do that.

5 · Taking a small stone from a historical site as a souvenir does no harm.

Model answer

I strongly disagree. One stone doesn't seem like much, but a popular site can get thousands of visitors a day, and if each of them took one, the walls would slowly disappear. A photo makes a better souvenir, and it costs the place nothing.

6 · Seeing a place in photos is almost as good as going there.

Model answer

I disagree. Photos can't give you the cold wind on your face or the feeling of standing where people lived hundreds of years ago. I still look at them first, though, to decide if a trip is worth the money.

Useful language
  • I strongly agree / I partly agree / I totally disagree, because …
  • It depends on whether …
  • From my own experience at the academy, …
  • I see your point, but …

Lead-in 3 · Talks and presentations — who gives them, and why?

5:00Each person takes one situation, then shares it with the group.

SB p. 136 Ex 1 · First tell a partner: do you ever give talks or presentations, in Vietnamese or in English? What are they about? Then look at the four situations below.

At work · a meeting on the hospital ward
  1. Question 1
    "Who gives short talks in a hospital, and who listens to them?"
  2. Question 2
    "Why would a young doctor have to present at the morning meeting?"
Model answer

Every morning on the ward, the doctor who was on night duty reports the new patients to the whole team, usually in two or three minutes. Junior doctors also present unusual cases, so everyone can learn from them. You need to be quick and sure of your numbers.

At school or university · the academy
  1. Question 1
    "What kinds of presentations do cadets give at the academy?"
  2. Question 2
    "Why do teachers ask you to present instead of only handing in a report?"
Model answer

We present group projects in seminars, and a few cadets present their research at the student science conference every year. Teachers can see straight away whether we really understand a topic or are just repeating the textbook. Standing up in front of a full lecture hall is good training for later, too.

At a meeting of a local club or town council
  1. Question 1
    "What could someone talk about at a meeting of a local club or the commune council?"
  2. Question 2
    "Would you give a health talk in your home village? What would it be about?"
Model answer

At council meetings in my village, people discuss things like a new road or repairs to the old communal house. Last summer a doctor from the district hospital gave a talk there about dengue fever, and people kept asking him questions for an hour. I'd happily do the same about first aid when I'm home on leave.

At a special occasion · a wedding or a unit event
  1. Question 1
    "Who usually makes a speech at a Vietnamese wedding?"
  2. Question 2
    "Which events at the academy need a short speech, and who gives it?"
Model answer

At a Vietnamese wedding, an older relative normally speaks for each family, and the speech can go on longer than the guests would like! At the academy there are speeches at graduation and when a commander retires. The best ones are short and a bit funny.

Useful language
  • People give talks at … to …
  • The speaker has to …, so …
  • I once had to present … and I felt …
  • The hardest part for me is …

Group 1 · Ancient history — people, objects and places

Core
People
an archaeologist /ˌɑːkiˈɒlədʒɪst/
a person who studies old buildings and objects to learn how people lived long ago
An archaeologist from the national museum showed our class how to clean a broken bowl with a soft brush.
a soldier /ˈsəʊldʒə(r)/
a member of an army who wears a uniform
Every cadet at our academy is a soldier as well as a future doctor.
a robber /ˈrɒbə(r)/
someone who takes money or things that belong to others, often by force
The robbers dug a tunnel under the wall at night and left before sunrise.
a collector /kəˈlektə(r)/
a person who buys and keeps a particular kind of thing, e.g. coins, stamps or old maps
A wealthy collector in Europe paid a fortune for a small stone head from Egypt.
an expert /ˈekspɜːt/
a person with special knowledge or skill in a subject
An expert looked at the coin and said it was about two thousand years old.
Objects
a tomb /tuːm/
a large grave, often a stone room, where an important person is buried
The king's tomb was cut deep into the rock so that nobody could find it.
a statue /ˈstætʃuː/
a stone, metal or wooden model of a person or an animal
A tall bronze statue of a general stands in front of the military museum.
a painting /ˈpeɪntɪŋ/
a picture made with colours on a wall, on paper or on cloth

a pot /pɒt/
a round container, often of clay, for cooking or for keeping food

gold /ɡəʊld/
a precious yellow metal used for coins, jewellery and masks

a historical object (item) /hɪˈstɒrɪkl ˈɒbdʒɪkt/ · /ˈaɪtəm/

The answer frame — 7 steps

Sample question: "Is it still worth visiting a historical place when you can see it online?"

1Point

Give your answer straight away, in one sentence.

To my mind, …

2Why

Add the main reason behind your answer.

The main reason is that …

3How

Explain how this works in practice.

When you …, you …

4Example

Give one specific case: a place, a time, a person.

Take …, for instance.

5Result

Show the effect of that one example.

Because of that, …

6Limitation

Accept a weak point or the opposite view.

Of course, …

7Evaluation

Weigh both sides and give your final view, without repeating the Point.

So on balance, …

Model answer · one paragraph with all seven steps

In the model answers: Point = step of the answer frame · frame language = opinion, reason, example, contrast, conclusion · unit vocabulary = Sheet 3 words and phrases (Groups 1–4)

PointTo my mind, it's still well worth visiting a historical place in person, even if you've already seen it online. WhyThe main reason is that being there uses all your senses, not just your eyes. HowWhen you're actually standing inside an old building, you notice things no camera picks up, like the smell of the wood. ExampleTake Đường Lâm, the ancient village in Sơn Tây, for instance. I'd scrolled through hundreds of photos before our class trip, but walking down its narrow lanes, between walls of rough reddish-brown stone, I could feel how old the place really was. ResultBecause of that afternoon, I spent the whole bus ride home reading about the village's history, which no video had ever made me do. LimitationOf course, a trip costs time and money, and online tours let people see sites that are too far away or too fragile for big crowds. EvaluationSo on balance, I'd say the internet is a great way to plan a visit or to reach places you'll never travel to, but it works best as a trailer for the real thing, not a replacement for it.

Where to use it
  • Speaking Part 1: keep to Point → Why → Example and finish in about half a minute.
  • Speaking Part 2: spread the seven steps over the cue-card prompts; let the Evaluation answer the last line, “and explain why …”.
  • Speaking Part 3: use every step, roughly one short sentence each, in about a minute.
  • Opinion paragraph (Sheet 8): Point = topic sentence with your opinion · Why/How + Example = each of the two main points · Limitation + a rebuttal = the concession, answered at once · a short final comment = concluding sentence, with no new idea.

Give each answer as one paragraph that moves through the seven steps: Point → Why → How → Example → Result → Limitation → Evaluation.

1 · Why do some people buy objects they know were stolen?

Model answer

PointMostly, I think, it's about prestige. WhyOwning a piece of the ancient world makes people feel important, especially when nobody else could buy one in a shop. HowThey usually deal through a middleman, so they never have to meet the robbers or see the damage. ExampleA friend's father, who runs a big company, keeps a small bronze statue in his office and once told me, almost proudly, that it had been dug up illegally. ResultHis foreign clients always ask about it, so it does exactly what he bought it for, and now he wants another. LimitationTo be fair, some buyers say they're rescuing objects that would otherwise be lost or broken. EvaluationI don't buy that excuse, though, and I'd rather see looted statues become embarrassing to own, not impressive.

2 · Should soldiers with guns protect archaeological sites?

Model answer

PointAt the big sites, yes, at least for now. WhyRobbers are often armed and well organised, and an archaeologist with a brush can't stop them. HowSoldiers don't need to be everywhere. They can guard the entrances at night. ExampleAt Umma, an ancient city in southern Iraq, eighteen guards kept robbers away until the 2003 war, when looters came in crowds and drove them off. ResultTwo months later, an archaeologist who arrived by helicopter counted at least two hundred people digging, and by 2008 the site was full of holes. LimitationStill, soldiers aren't magic. Abu Sir al Malaq has them now, but in 2012 Amal met three men stealing from a tomb. EvaluationOn balance, soldiers buy time, but in the long run cameras, local guards and tougher laws matter more.

3 · Which is worse: stealing one object, or destroying hundreds while looking for it?

Model answer

PointDestroying hundreds is far worse, even though the theft gets more attention. WhyA stolen statue still exists and might be returned one day, but a smashed pot is gone for good. HowArchaeologists learn a lot from where things lie, so careless digging wipes out that information too. ExampleThe Euphronios krater, a famous painted Greek vase, was dug out of an Etruscan tomb by robbers in 1971, and nobody knows exactly which tomb. ResultThe vase went back to Italy in 2008, but the rest of that grave's contents vanished with the robbers. LimitationOf course, the sale pays for the damage, so the two crimes can't really be separated. EvaluationI'd still punish those who dig and smash more heavily than someone caught with one stolen piece, since their damage can't be undone.

4 · Will the problem of stealing history ever be solved? What would help most?

Model answer

PointI doubt it'll ever disappear completely, but it can definitely shrink. WhyAs long as people pay large amounts of money, someone will dig at night. HowWhat would help most is making it risky, not just illegal, to sell or buy looted pieces. ExampleIn 2011, Sotheby's pulled a thousand-year-old Khmer statue from a sale after Cambodia said it had been hacked off its base at Koh Ker. ResultThe seller never got the millions it was expected to fetch, and in 2014 the statue went home. LimitationThen again, laws only work where governments can check sites and borders, and poorer countries often can't. EvaluationSo I'd spend money on checking where objects come from before a sale, because a statue nobody can sell is one nobody bothers to steal.

Step 1 Know the opinion paragraph

GoalTake a model opinion paragraph apart: the job of each sentence, the three parts and the six features.
Why this worksLearners write a text type better after they have taken a good example apart and named its parts.
Know this
  • An opinion paragraph answers one question (do you agree?) in about 8–10 sentences.
  • Part 1 · Topic sentence: the topic + your opinion + the controlling idea (what you will prove).
  • Part 2 · Supporting sentences: two main points, each with minor details (an explanation and an example), then one concession + rebuttal.
  • Part 3 · Concluding sentence: your opinion once more, worded differently, with no new idea.
  • Six features of an effective paragraph: clear organisation · full development · unity · coherence and cohesion · vocabulary · sentences.
